# Pattern: Defensive Asymmetry

- Pattern ID: PAT-0001
- Pattern name: Defensive Asymmetry — Five Defensive Pieces, One Offensive Weapon
- Pattern type: Structural / rhetorical
- Strength of evidence: Strong
- Approval status: Draft — pending Chase review

---

## Passages included

- Ephesians 6:14–17 (PASS-0001)

---

## Exact shared features

In Paul's list of six armor pieces, five are purely defensive (belt, breastplate, shoes, shield, helmet) and one is a weapon (the sword). This 5:1 ratio is not incidental — it is structurally encoded in the text and confirmed by the Greek:

- The five defensive pieces are all things worn or carried for protection: they absorb, deflect, or withstand attack
- The sword (*machaira*, G3162) is the only item used to strike
- The shield's function is explicitly stated: "quench all the fiery darts of the wicked" — defensive
- The word *panoplia* (G3833, "whole armor") would typically include a lance — Paul omits it. He keeps only the short sword

---

## What this pattern may mean

*(Interpretation — labeled as such)*

The asymmetry suggests that Paul's model of spiritual warfare is primarily about **holding ground**, not taking it. The goal stated three times in the passage is to "stand" or "withstand" (vv. 11, 13, 14). The armor does not make the believer a conqueror or an aggressor; it makes them someone who cannot be moved.

The single offensive weapon — the sword of the Spirit — is a *machaira*, a short, close-contact weapon. Even the offensive capacity Paul gives the believer is intimate and precise rather than distant or overwhelming. And it is *rhema* (specific, living utterance) rather than *logos* (abstract doctrine) — the word goes out in a specific moment, against a specific attack.

---

## Disconfirming or complicating passages

- The Book of Mormon and D&C passages use similar armor language — whether they share this defensive asymmetry needs verification in later loops
- Some interpretations emphasize the "sword of the Spirit" as the believer's primary tool for missionary work (spreading the gospel), which would shift the offensive weight considerably — this is an open question
- 2 Corinthians 10:3–5 uses warfare imagery that sounds more actively offensive ("pulling down strongholds") — whether this modifies or contextualizes the Ephesians picture is worth studying
