## Session: 2026-10-26 (Loop 4 — Bible cross-reference passages)

**Phase:** 1 — Study and Research

**Question studied:**
What other passages in the Bible use armor imagery, and how do they relate to Ephesians 6?

**Sources added:**

**Completed work:**
- Fetched and verified five passages from Church website
- Created PASS-0002 (Isaiah 59:14–21), PASS-0003 (Isaiah 11:5), PASS-0004 (1 Thess. 5:8), PASS-0005 (Romans 13:12,14), PASS-0006 (2 Cor. 10:3–5)
- Created PAT-0002 (The Wearer Shift — God/Christ → believers)
- Created PAT-0003 (Pauline Development — armor metaphor grows across letters)

**Key findings:**
1. Isaiah 59:17 is almost certainly Paul's source text — breastplate of righteousness and helmet of salvation appear verbatim; God puts on armor because no human intercessor exists; the same chapter ends with the Redeemer coming to Zion
2. Paul strips the retributive elements from Isaiah's armor (vengeance, zeal) and adds belt, shoes, shield, and sword
3. Isaiah 11:5 shows Christ/the Branch wearing the belt of righteousness and faithfulness — directly cross-referenced to Ephesians 6:14 in KJV footnotes
4. 1 Thessalonians 5:8 (~50–51 AD) is Paul's earlier, less-developed version: only two pieces, faith assigned to breastplate, "hope of salvation" as helmet — Ephesians reassigns faith to shield and drops the "hope of" qualifier
5. Romans 13:12,14 equates putting on the armor with putting on Christ himself — the armor = Christ's character
6. 2 Corinthians 10:3–5 uses warfare language that is more offensive than Ephesians 6 (pulling down strongholds) — complementary, not contradictory

2. Should the site note the 1 Thessalonians comparison ("hope of salvation" → "salvation")?

## Session: 2026-10-26 (Loop 3 — Greek and Roman context)

**Phase:** 1 — Study and Research

**Questions studied:**
- What do the Greek words behind each armor piece reveal about Paul's intended meaning?
- What did Roman military armor actually look like in Paul's time?

**Sources approved this session:**
- SRC-0005: Blue Letter Bible (Greek lexicon, Strong's)
- SRC-0006: Wikipedia — Roman military personal equipment

**Completed work:**
- Fetched Greek lexicon entries for: panoplia (G3833), thyreos (G2375), machaira (G3162), rhema (G4487), perikephalaia (G4030) — all via SRC-0005
- Fetched Roman military equipment descriptions via SRC-0006
- Updated PASS-0001 "Important words and phrases" section with full verified Greek/Roman data
- Created PAT-0001: Defensive Asymmetry pattern record

**Key findings:**
1. *Panoplia* = "all weapons/armor" — Paul uses it TWICE; a standard panoplia also included a lance and greaves, which Paul deliberately omits, and adds belt and boots instead
2. *Thyreos* (shield) = shaped like a DOOR (from *thyra*) — the large oblong body-covering shield, not the small round aspis; appears only once in the entire NT
3. *Machaira* (sword) = a short sword/dirk, not a long blade — close-range and precise; Roman gladius equivalent
4. *Rhema* (word of God in the sword verse) = a specific spoken utterance in a particular moment, NOT logos (the abstract divine Word) — the sword is living and situational, not a static reference
5. *Perikephalaia* (helmet) = literally "encirclement of the head" (*peri* + *kephale*)
6. Paul's list OMITS the lance (aggressive throwing weapon) and greaves (shin armor) from a standard panoplia; ADDS belt and boots — both standing-and-readiness pieces
7. Roman context: cingulum (belt) was the foundational piece that held everything else; scutum (shield) was large enough to interlock with others in tortoise formation; gladius was 60cm short sword worn on right hip

**Pattern created:**
- PAT-0001: Defensive Asymmetry (5 defensive pieces : 1 offensive weapon; all three stated goals are "to stand")

**Decisions needed from Chase:**
